This wrist goes on the end of a Barrett WAM. The operator straps their forearm into its cuff and holds the handle, and the WAM and wrist together lead a second WAM. After the end-effector wrist, it went back to a forearm cuff, now with cable drives on every joint.

It has four joints. Three follow the human wrist: pronation–supination (PS), flexion–extension (FE) and radial–ulnar deviation (RU). The fourth, which I call ATT for active attachment, sits between the wrist and its mount and turns the whole thing, cuff included.

How the cables run

The PS and FE motors both sit at the bottom of the wrist. Each drives a small first stage into a large ring centred on the PS axis, with the PS ring stacked on the FE ring and the forearm passing up through both, so the PS axis runs along the forearm.

PS ends there, at 34.3:1 overall. FE has two more stages: stage 3 is an arc that carries the drive up the side of the gimbal, and stage 4 sits on the FE axis, for 12.3:1 overall. RU has its own motor and two stages at 7.6:1. A quasi-direct-drive motor turns the ATT joint through a large pulley on the mount. PS and FE use T-Motor GB54-2 gimbal motors, RU a CubeMars GL60, and all of them run on mjbots moteus controllers.

Driving each motor

In each clip, one motor drives the wrist on its own. Motors 1, 2 and 3 are PS, FE and RU.

Torque at each joint

The transmission maps the three wrist motor torques to joint torques:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \tau_{\text{PS}} \\ \tau_{\text{FE}} \\ \tau_{\text{RU}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 34.3 & 0 & 0 \\ 12.3 & 12.3 & 0 \\ -44 & -44 & 7.6 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \tau_{m1} \\ \tau_{m2} \\ \tau_{m3} \end{bmatrix} \]

PS only sees motor 1. FE sees the sum of motors 1 and 2, so any torque motor 1 puts on PS also lands on FE, and motor 2 has to cancel it if FE is supposed to stay still.

Measuring the motors myself, I got 0.45 N·m continuous and 0.60 N·m peak from each GB54-2, and 0.60 N·m continuous and 1.75 N·m peak from the GL60. Pushing the peak values through the matrix, one joint at a time:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_{\text{PS}} &= 34.3 \times 0.60 = 20.6\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m} \\ \tau_{\text{FE}} &= 12.3 \times (0.60 + 0.60) = 14.8\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m} \\ \tau_{\text{RU}} &= 7.6 \times 1.75 = 13.3\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m} \end{aligned} \]

The RU figure counts motor 3 alone.

The bottom row is the problem. Each newton-metre from motor 1 or motor 2 puts 44 N·m on RU with the opposite sign, almost six times what motor 3 gets through its own 7.6:1 drive. With motor 3 at its 1.75 N·m peak, motors 1 and 2 cancel all of it once

\[ |\tau_{m1} + \tau_{m2}| = \frac{7.6 \times 1.75}{44} \approx 0.30\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m}, \]

which is half the peak of one GB54-2.

Why the ATT joint is there

A human wrist covers a small part of what the WAM’s wrist can do. Mapping both in yaw, pitch and roll, the leader reaches 20.3% of the WAM wrist’s orientations without the ATT joint and 43.3% with it.

Mostly, the ATT joint deals with misalignment between my arm and the WAM. With it active I move my body much less, and the wrist is far less tiring to use. It also lets the leader make better use of the follower’s wrist. The follower can twist its end effector purely about joint 7, but a human wrist can only make that pure twist with the hand in line with the forearm, and without fingers, that single-axis twist is what you need for threading tasks.

The ATT joint isn’t free, though. It complicates the mechanism and the control, and makes it harder to tell by eye whether leader and follower are in the same pose. It’s also part of the orientation control loop. We weight its share of the commanded torque so it doesn’t render much of the correction force on the leader, but it’s fair to ask why it’s in that loop at all.

On the WAM

Fixing the first build

The first build had five mechanical problems:

  • Cable sliding off stage 1 on PS and FE. The cable could slide off the ends of the pulley. I gave those stages a larger range so the mechanism never moves out to the ends, and added dowel pins about 0.5% of a revolution in from each end to keep the cable aligned and stop it slipping, as the WAM’s wrist does.
  • Tensioning FE stage 4. There’s very little room there, so the tensioner had little travel, and under higher tension the crimps got ripped off in use. Laser-cut parts, e-clips and wrapping the cable made it more reliable and gave it more travel, and I switched to a loop and a double wrap, like joint 1 of the WAM.
  • Bolts backing out of the RU stage 1 pulley. I didn’t have the right hardware for the first build, so I used Loctite as a stopgap. It didn’t hold, and I ended up replacing it with an aluminum bolt.
  • Motor controller mounts on PS and FE. The controllers sat outside the encoder’s working magnet gap (1.5 to 2.5 mm, with 2 mm best), and the mount wasn’t very stable. Both made the position reading noisier and less accurate, which hurt the field-oriented control. A thicker, stiffer mount fixed both and put the gap at 2 mm.
  • Cable creep on RU stage 2. That cable is about 1 m long and stretched at least 10 mm with use, about 1%, which the small tensioner couldn’t take up until I gave it more travel.

Other problems come from the kinematics themselves. Moving FE tends to move PS as well, so the handle doesn’t always go where the user is pushing it. That coupling also lets the motors fight each other: when you backdrive the wrist, you have to hold a counter-torque to stop motion you didn’t ask for. FE stage 4 also has an “increaser” in it that doesn’t make sense in a device like this, and getting rid of it means rearranging some of the parts.

Control

At first we had a non-zero kd in the moteus drives’ own PID loop, so each drive applied a hidden inner derivative term, −kd·ω, on top of our outer PD controllers, even in torque mode. I think the extra term amplified velocity noise and made the system unstable. Setting kd to 0 got rid of it.

The orientation controller is very sensitive to its gains, so I had to run them very low on the leader, which limits how much force feedback it can give.

On the moteus side, I made a few changes:

  • I swapped the fdcanusb adapter for a PEAK PCAN-PCI Express FD card. The fdcanusb runs at USB 2.0 full speed, 12 Mbit/s with 1 ms frame scheduling, and shows up as a serial device, so every CAN frame goes through the kernel’s USB and TTY layers. The PEAK card sits on PCIe with a native kernel driver and direct CAN frame queues.
  • I raised --cal-bw-hz from the default 200 Hz to 800 Hz, which stiffens the current loop so torque can change faster.
  • I updated to the moteus firmware with the new hall-sensor pipeline, which times transitions at very low speed and uses a PLL at higher speed. Its velocity estimate is much less spiky, and after the update our control loops saw far less derivative noise.

Using it

Range is still the biggest limit, and without your fingers most tasks are awkward. The ATT joint’s extra range does make tabletop tasks possible with the elbow down, which the three-joint wrists can’t do, but more range on the leader would make it much more usable.

I’m also not sure force feedback on all three anatomical joints helps. It feels a bit awkward, and PS motion often makes the arm slip in the cuff, which throws the user out of line with the WAM. For most tasks, feedback on PS seems less important than on FE and RU, so a later version could drop it there.

The elbow is the other open problem. Ideally the plane through my upper arm and forearm matches the plane through the WAM’s. That’s easy to keep in gravity mode, but in teleoperation the WAM’s higher apparent impedance damps the motion and keeping the two planes together gets much harder. I ended up adjusting the elbow with my other arm or moving my body to compensate.

Update, April 2026

The WHAM wrist took a different approach to the same problems: just two haptic joints, PS and FE, with far more PS range than this one, a thumb joystick for the follower’s last joint, and a passive joint at the forearm so the operator’s elbow doesn’t have to line up with the WAM’s.
