Since 2022. Undergraduate research assistant on a lab team.

A teleoperated robot for disaster zones and other places too dangerous to send a person. A 7‑DOF Barrett WAM rides on a Clearpath Husky. The operator drives it from a second WAM and feels the forces the robot meets, while a 360° camera on the robot feeds a VR headset. We’re one of eight finalist teams in the IDEaS (Innovation for Defence Excellence and Security) challenge, which funded it with a $500K grant.

My first concept had the WAM simply sitting on top of the Husky.

The team, in the Computer Vision and Robotics Group at the University of Alberta, was Dylan Miller, Faezeh Haghverd, Jakub Piwowarczyk, Mark Fiala, Martin Jagersand and me. My part was the mobile base, the sensors and ROS.

Haptic teleoperation

On the operator side, a 4‑DOF WAM carries our 3‑DOF cable-driven wrist exoskeleton, matching the robot arm’s seven joints one for one. The wrist is light and runs on low-cost BLDC motor controllers. Because it’s cable driven, joint torque is a linear function of motor output, so the force the operator feels is scaled correctly.

The two arms are coupled joint to joint: each joint runs a PD position loop with its twin on the other arm as the setpoint, so when the robot pushes on something, the operator’s arm pushes back. Either arm can lead. How stiff that coupling can get while staying stable depends on the communication latency between the two arms.

The mobile base

For the real robot I redesigned and built the Husky’s chassis and body panels so the WAM sits lower than in the concept video, which brings the centre of mass down. The WAM mounts to the chassis through milled aluminum parts.

Cameras and the headset

The operator sees the scene through a FLIR Ladybug2 spherical camera on a mast, which captures 360° images at 10 Hz and sends them over UDP. We project each spherical image onto a virtual cube and show it in an Oculus Rift headset. Two FLIR Blackfly cameras add higher-resolution views to the front and rear.

Mapping, ROS and Wi‑Fi

I integrated the sensors in ROS (the LiDAR, the Ladybug2 and Blackfly cameras, an IMU and GPS) along with URDF models of the whole robot. With SLAM running on the Hokuyo UTM‑30LX‑EW LiDAR, the robot can map a floor and drive itself around it. I also got some legacy LiDAR hardware running on ROS Noetic, chasing down hardware and communication faults with an oscilloscope and serial diagnostics.

Update, August 2024: the IDEaS demonstration

We demonstrated the robot to the Department of National Defence. The demonstration, which I helped present, was a search-and-rescue scenario with five tasks: remote tabletop manipulation, zero-tolerance precision manipulation, remote door opening, autonomous room navigation and interactive scene awareness.
