title: A haptic lightsaber game date: 2026-10 topics: haptics, games, software, control math: yes summary: Sword fighting on a WAM with the haptic wrist, where clashing blades push back harder near the hilt than near the tip, played at Barrett’s booth at IROS 2026. image: images/lightsaber.jpg ---

Built in 2026 at Barrett Technology.

I wrote a sword-fighting game for a WAM with the haptic wrist. The wrist’s handle is the hilt: the arm sets where it is, and the wrist sets where the blade points, roll included. When my blade meets the droid’s, the arm and the wrist push back, harder for a hit near the hilt than near the tip. Visitors played it at Barrett’s booth at IROS 2026 in Pittsburgh.

The game and its haptics are C++ on libbarrett, inside the arm’s 500 Hz control loop. Game state goes out over UDP at 100 Hz to display-only viewers, one on the desktop and, later, one for the Meta Quest 3 with stereo and head tracking. There are two modes, fruit slicing and sparring, each with five difficulty levels.

How the force is rendered

Every 2 ms the loop reads the joint angles, and libbarrett’s forward kinematics give the handle’s position \(\mathbf p\), velocity \(\dot{\mathbf p}\) and orientation. The blade runs along \(\hat{\mathbf b}\), one axis of the wrist frame, from 4 cm to 50 cm out from the handle. The game steps the fruit and the droid, checks for contact and returns a single force \(\mathbf F\) and moment \(\mathbf M\) about the handle.

Several contact forces depend on the blade’s speed at the contact point, which with a wrist is mostly rotation. The game estimates the angular velocity from how the blade axis and the edge axis \(\hat{\mathbf e}\) turned since the last step, smooths it with a first-order filter that cuts off near 13 Hz, and gets the velocity of any point \(\mathbf c\) on the blade from it:

\[ \boldsymbol\omega_k = \boldsymbol\omega_{k-1} + 0.15\left(\frac{\hat{\mathbf b}_{k-1}\times\hat{\mathbf b}_k + \hat{\mathbf e}_{k-1}\times\hat{\mathbf e}_k}{2\,\Delta t} - \boldsymbol\omega_{k-1}\right) \] \[ \mathbf v(\mathbf c) = \dot{\mathbf p} + \boldsymbol\omega \times (\mathbf c - \mathbf p) \]

Finding the contact

Both blades are capsules with the 12 mm radius of their white cores (the glow doesn’t count), so they touch when their axes come within 24 mm. A fast cut can cross the red blade between two 2 ms steps, so both blades are swept between steps in up to 16 samples about 8 mm apart.

The droid’s shell is 11 fitted ellipsoids, and its arms are capsules: 18 mm for the housings, 10 mm for the wrist rods, 29 mm for the joints and 25 mm for the hands. The game takes the first surface along my blade, so the red blade screens whatever is behind it. Fruit are spheres, tested against the closest point on the blade’s axis.

The room is scenery. Its only force is a soft fence, 1100 N/m and 14 N·s/m past each face, that keeps the handle inside a 45 × 55 × 55 cm box, the same play box as Barrett’s haptic_world demo.

Blade against blade

If the blade-on-blade force were a spring on the overlap, a hard enough push would carry my blade through the red one, and once the axes crossed the spring would flip and push me the rest of the way. So the game keeps a proxy of my blade that can’t enter the red one. When the blades first touch, it fixes the contact normal \(\hat{\mathbf n}\). Each step after that, it finds the smallest shift \(\delta\) along \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) that moves the proxy clear of the red capsule, by bisection with 32 halvings, keeping the measured orientation. The proxy stays on the side it came in from until I pull back or slide off the rounded end of either blade, so pushing through a block can’t cut the droid behind it. It’s the finite-coupling proxy from CHAI3D’s haptic rendering notes, reduced to translation.

The handle is coupled to the proxy by a spring and a damper:

\[ \mathbf F_c = g(r)\,\big[K\,\delta + D\,\max(0,\,-v_n)\big]\,\hat{\mathbf n}, \qquad \mathbf M_c = (\mathbf c - \mathbf p)\times\mathbf F_c \] \[ g(r) = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{r_0^2}{r^2}\right), \qquad r = \lVert \mathbf c - \mathbf p \rVert \]

with \(K = 1400\) N/m, \(D = 22\) N·s/m and \(r_0 = 0.22\) m. Here \(\mathbf c\) is the contact point on my blade and \(v_n\) is my blade’s speed relative to the red one along \(\hat{\mathbf n}\). The damper acts only while the blades are closing, so the contact can push but never pull. There’s no friction along the blade and no added vibration, so a held press is a steady push.

The \(g(r)\) term is there for the wrist’s sake. For small rotations, a contact at distance \(r\) from the handle feels to the wrist like a rotational spring of stiffness \(K r^2\). Near the tip, 49 cm out, that’s 330 N·m/rad, about five times the value at 22 cm. On September 24 the wrist rumbled hard on contacts near the tip, so beyond 22 cm both gains now fall off as \((r_0/r)^2\). Past that point the wrist feels the same 68 N·m/rad wherever the hit lands, and the hand feels a softer push the farther out it is.

Fruit and the droid’s body

A fruit is cut when the blade crosses it faster than 0.22 m/s relative to the fruit, in any direction. Slower than that, it rests on the blade on a soft spring (550 N/m, 8 N·s/m) and gets shoved around through its mass. A cut is a drag against \(\mathbf v_\perp\), the blade’s velocity relative to the fruit with the part along the blade removed:

\[ \mathbf F_{\text{cut}} = -\left[\frac{0.28\,c\,\big(0.3 + 0.7\sqrt{\hat d}\,\big)}{\lVert\mathbf v_\perp\rVert + 0.45} + \frac{p\,\sin(\pi t / t_r)}{\lVert\mathbf v_\perp\rVert + 0.12}\right]\mathbf v_\perp \]

Speeds are in m/s. The first term is the flesh: it grows with the depth \(\hat d\), as a fraction of the fruit’s radius, and levels off for fast cuts so the fruit still feel different during a quick wrist flick. The second is a half-sine rind break over the first \(t_r = 24\) ms. The force eases in over 8 ms and out over the last 45 ms of the cut, fades in over the first 10 mm of depth, stops as soon as the blade leaves the fruit and is capped at 10 N per fruit. Like every force here it acts at the contact point, so its moment \((\mathbf c - \mathbf p)\times\mathbf F_{\text{cut}}\) twists the wrist harder for a cut near the tip.

From level 4 up there are bombs. Touching one sets off a 30 Hz buzz that starts at 13 N on the arm and 1.1 N·m on the wrist and dies away over 0.32 s.

The droid’s body works the same way with harder numbers. A sideways cut faster than 0.28 m/s gets a drag of 14 N·s/m for 90 ms through an arm or 24 N·s/m for 130 ms through the chassis, an 8 or 10 N pop at the shell, and a light 65 Hz rattle at 12% of the drag, capped at 12 N. A slower brush only clangs. A tip pushed into the shell meets a spring of 450 N/m and 6 N·s/m, capped at 8 N, until it’s 6 mm in and still moving inward faster than 6 cm/s. Then it goes through.

Adding it up

Everything is summed, each force with its moment about the handle, and the total is scaled as one wrench so the ratio of force to moment survives the limit:

\[ s = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{26\ \mathrm{N}}{\lVert\mathbf F\rVert},\ \frac{2\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m}}{\lVert\mathbf M\rVert}\right), \qquad (\mathbf F,\,\mathbf M) \leftarrow s\,(\mathbf F,\,\mathbf M) \]

For one contact square to the blade, the largest force is \(\min(26\ \mathrm{N},\ 2\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m}/r)\). The moment limit takes over 7.7 cm from the handle, nearly the whole blade, so for most hits the contact’s distance out sets how hard the arm can push. That 2 N·m is about a quarter of the wrist’s 8.4 N·m peak.

Joint torques

libbarrett maps the wrench to joint torques with the transposes of the linear and angular parts of the tool Jacobian:

\[ \boldsymbol\tau = J_v(\mathbf q)^{\mathsf T}\,\mathbf F + J_\omega(\mathbf q)^{\mathsf T}\,\mathbf M \]

With the haptic wrist, the game’s handle point is the wrist centre, where the three wrist axes meet. Turning a wrist joint doesn’t move that point, so the wrist columns of \(J_v\) are zero and the wrist motors carry only moment. The force goes to the four arm joints, which also take part of the moment. J5 on this arm has no motor, so its entry is dropped. The whole vector is then scaled once more so that no joint passes its cap and the torque keeps its direction:

\[ \boldsymbol\tau \leftarrow \boldsymbol\tau\,\min\!\left(1,\ \min_i \frac{\tau_{\max,i}}{\lvert\tau_i\rvert}\right) \]

The caps are 25, 20, 15 and 15 N·m on J1 to J4 and 2 N·m on each wrist joint. libbarrett’s gravity compensation is added outside these limits. On a plain four-joint WAM with no wrist, the blade’s aim is fixed in the room and only \(J_v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf F\) is sent, since a moment would twist the arm toward a rotation the virtual blade can’t make.

The rest of the safety lives outside the game. The safety board faults the arm if any of J1 to J4 passes 4 rad/s, a limit raised from 3 after a fast forearm roll tripped it at 3.3 rad/s. The program also polls the safety mode at 10 Hz, and if the arm leaves active mode for any reason it turns the haptics off for the rest of the run. Otherwise forces computed while the arm sat idle would come back at full strength on the next activation, wherever the arm had sagged to.

Opponents

On the arm there are two. The sparring droid hovers in front of you, holding a red saber in two arms that grip the hilt at two points. It only defends: it watches my blade, predicts where a cut is going and moves a crossing guard into its path. The guard is limited in how fast it can move and turn, so a quick change of angle can still get around it. Higher levels read a cut earlier, protect the hands better and move the guard a little quicker, but the forces are the same at every level. A cut that gets around the guard can take off an arm, which slows the droid’s reactions, or destroy the chassis, after which a replacement drops in.

The booth build adds a sentry, which every so often floats out of the door at the back and fires slow bolts at you. A blade held in the path knocks a bolt aside. Swinging into it, or bringing the blade into line within 240 ms of its arrival, sends it back at the sentry. A deflection is a 3.5 N half-sine push on the handle lasting 50 ms.

The headset has more opponents, in a controller practice mode with no robot at all: a Quest controller is the hilt, the same Python game runs the fight, and the controller vibrates on clashes, blocks and cuts in place of force feedback. A walking droid with one saber, a four-armed droid with up to four blades and a tall assassin droid all attack as well as defend. Each attack starts with a wind-up aimed at whatever part of you your blade leaves open. Hold a blade in its path to block it, or counter-swing fast to parry and stagger the droid. The middle level chains two-hit combos, and the top level three, with feints. The bodies are rigs of rigid bones posed every 1/240 s, with planted feet, two-bone IK and hit volumes that match what’s drawn. Some of their habits come from a recorded practice session in which the player held the tip 1.1 to 1.2 m ahead of the eyes, and the better droids now measure your reach and stand just outside it. None of this is connected to the robot, so on the arm you still fight the hovering droid.

The headset viewer

The headset viewer runs in the Meta Quest Browser with WebXR and Three.js, so there’s no app to install. A small Python bridge on the computer takes the same UDP telemetry as the desktop viewer and passes the newest sample to the headset over a WebSocket. It never opens the CAN bus or sends anything toward the robot. Over USB, adb reverse lets the headset load the page from localhost, which WebXR accepts as a secure context without a certificate, and a clock handshake lets it drop frames that arrive late after a Wi-Fi stall.

The room and the blade are drawn at full scale around the real handle, so they have to line up with it. In passthrough I hold the controller’s grip still on the saber’s grip and pull the trigger at three spots at least 15 cm apart. The fit solves only for a turn about the vertical and a translation, keeping gravity and metre scale, and is rejected if the residual is over 25 mm. Then the trigger switches from passthrough to the virtual room.

During a block the headset draws the proxy blade stopped against the red one, even when my hand has pushed on past it. That’s the blade the force comes from. If the telemetry stops for 250 ms, the saber, the arm and the targets disappear and a message says the link is lost. That only affects the display; you still stop the robot with the WAM’s own controls.

A spectator page for a TV next to the player shows the same room from broadcast-style cameras, with a mannequin standing in for the player, and can record vertical clips.
