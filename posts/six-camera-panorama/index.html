A 360° camera from six $20 action cameras

April 2023 · Computer vision, Machining, Electronics

This is my final project for CMPUT 428, the computer vision course at the University of Alberta, done with Justin Rozeboom in April 2023. We have two goals: build a cheap 360° camera that can drive around on a robot, and adapt structure from motion to a cylindrical camera instead of the usual pinhole one. I build the camera and the robot setup and do the calibration and stitching. Justin leads the reprojection and structure-from-motion part. The code is on GitHub at JustinValentine/panoramic-image-SFM.

The cameras

I already know action cameras, so I look for cheap ones and pick the Jadfezy Action Camera FHD 1080P 12MP off AliExpress, about $20 each with an advertised 110° field of view. We choose quickly because of the deadline, and with more research they turn out to be a poor fit. The real field of view is about 70° across the sensor and 40° along its height. Each camera also comes with a waterproof case, batteries, mounts and a screen on the back. We don’t use any of it, and that money could have gone into a better sensor. Small USB cameras would suit the project better. They advertise wider fields of view, and since they’re smaller they can sit closer together, which helps with parallax.

The bigger job is getting six separate cameras to behave as one. Each camera has to be switched on and then put into USB camera mode through its menu, which is 18 button presses for all six. My plan is to wire the on buttons of all six together, then the select buttons, then the down buttons, so one press works every camera. After more experimenting I find I could have just soldered each on button closed, so the cameras start as soon as they get power. In the final version the on buttons are shorted by twisting together the wires I’ve spent so long attaching. I also try to remove a button from one camera and rip a solder pad off the PCB, so I leave the other five alone. Starting all six cameras in USB camera mode now takes 7 presses.

Choosing a layout

We try several layouts first. Each has its own problems, and most need more cameras to cover the full circle.

We settle on a hexagon with one camera per side. It approximates a cylindrical camera reasonably well, and neighbouring views overlap enough to stitch. Each camera sits off-centre on its side, placed so that every point where two neighbouring fields of view cross is the same distance from both cameras.

The housing

The cameras sit in a laser-cut wood housing on top of the robot. Wood is cheap and easy to work with, and the laser cutter places each camera precisely, which matters for stitching. My first cut file has a big mistake: the laser would have cut off every tab. I fix it, cut the parts and assemble them, and the result looks like a birdhouse.

Power and driving

The robot is an iRobot Create 2, a low-cost differential-drive robot made for development, which we already have. All six cameras can stream over one USB cable, but together they draw too much power for it. I put them on a powered USB hub fed by a USB power bank, so the cable going to the computer only carries data. A Raspberry Pi 3B on the robot reads a PS4 controller and sends wheel velocities to the Create 2 over serial. There’s also an SSD for the images and video, and two power banks, one for the Pi and one for the cameras.

To finish it I paint the wood, put cable sleeves on the USB cables, raise the cameras on a cardboard tube and make four mounting points on the robot for the housing.

Calibration

The lenses have strong barrel distortion, so every frame has to be undistorted before stitching. We calibrate with OpenCV from photos of a checkerboard (8 × 6 inner corners) held at different positions and angles in front of the camera.

My first attempt uses cv.calibrateCamera(), OpenCV’s standard model. It throws away so much of the image around the edges that stitching would be impossible. The second uses OpenCV’s fisheye module: cv2.fisheye.calibrate() for the intrinsics and cv2.fisheye.estimateNewCameraMatrixForUndistortRectify() to undistort. It takes a lot more effort to set up and works much better.

The fisheye model maps a point \((X, Y, Z)\) in the camera frame to a pixel through the angle \(\theta\) between its ray and the optical axis:

\[ a = \frac{X}{Z}, \qquad b = \frac{Y}{Z}, \] \[ r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \qquad \theta = \arctan r, \] \[ \theta_d = \theta\,\big(1 + k_1\theta^2 + k_2\theta^4 + k_3\theta^6 + k_4\theta^8\big), \] \[ u = f_x\,\frac{\theta_d}{r}\,a + c_x, \qquad v = f_y\,\frac{\theta_d}{r}\,b + c_y . \]

Calibration finds the camera matrix \(K\) (focal lengths and principal point, with skew fixed at zero) and the distortion coefficients \(D = (k_1, k_2, k_3, k_4)\). At 1280 × 720 we get

\[ K = \begin{bmatrix} 841.7 & 0 & 649.7 \\ 0 & 843.3 & 345.2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \] \[ D = (-0.157,\ 0.657,\ -1.178,\ 0.751). \]

Undistorting goes the other way. For each pixel of the output image, OpenCV takes the ray through it, runs that ray through the model above to find where it lands in the raw frame, and samples there. The balance setting, 0.9 in our script, controls how much of the original frame is kept versus cropped. The raw frames come out upside down, so the script flips each one first, and it applies the same \(K\) and \(D\) to all six cameras.

To check the result I print 16 checkerboards, arrange them into one large board and photograph it with one of the cameras. After undistortion the grid lines come out straight.

Stitching

We line up our first panoramas by hand in Photoshop, and they come out poorly. By hand there’s no consistent way to deal with the distortion and parallax, or to work out how each frame relates to the next.

Next we try OpenCV’s cv2.Stitcher_create() in panorama mode, which gives much better results. It detects and matches features between frames, estimates the transformations between them and blends them with multi-band blending, which hides many of the seams that parallax causes.

Parallax is the main limit. The six cameras don’t share an optical centre, so nearby objects shift between neighbouring views and can’t line up exactly. The Create 2 is built for indoor use, and indoors things tend to be close to the camera, which makes it worse. A simple fix is to turn the robot 30°, half the spacing between cameras, and capture again. That gives twelve views instead of six with much more overlap between them, so the stitcher has more matches to work with, including some that parallax affects less.

Projecting onto a cylinder

The stitcher’s output looks good, but I don’t know how geometrically accurate it is compared with a true cylindrical camera. PhotoStitcher, a photo-stitching program, lets you pick a projection type, and that gets me thinking about it. I implement the ProjectOntoCylinder function from Kedia Rahul’s “Panorama Stitching” guide and run every frame through it before stitching.

Each column of the output is an angle around the cylinder, \(\theta = (x' - c_x)/f\), and each row is a height on it. A ray at angle \(\theta\) hits the flat image plane at \(f\tan\theta\) from the centre, and at that angle the plane is \(1/\cos\theta\) times farther away than the cylinder, so heights scale by the same factor. For each output pixel \((x', y')\), the code samples the original image at

\[ x = f\tan\!\left(\frac{x' - c_x}{f}\right) + c_x, \] \[ y = \frac{y' - c_y}{\cos\!\big((x' - c_x)/f\big)} + c_y , \]

with bilinear interpolation, then crops off the empty columns at the sides.

The warp needs the focal length \(f\) in pixels. Converting from the lens focal length in millimetres takes the sensor width, so I take apart a broken camera and measure its sensor with calipers. It’s 3.6 mm wide, covering the 1280-pixel image width, so

\[ f_{\text{px}} = \frac{f_{\text{mm}}}{3.6\ \text{mm}} \times 1280 . \]

Here is the same outdoor capture as the panorama at the top of the post, at the two earlier stages. Adding the cylindrical projection on top of calibration gives the version at the top.

Testing the cylindrical model

To measure how well the panoramas match a cylindrical camera, I mark 45° increments on the floor around the robot and 20 cm increments up a board. The plan is to photograph the board at each angle, stitch, and compare where the marks land with where the model puts them, since I know their real positions. The walls in that room are too plain, though. The stitcher can’t find enough matching features between frames, and I never get a panorama good enough to measure.

Reprojection and structure from motion

Justin Rozeboom leads this part. The common structure-from-motion algorithms work with flat image planes, so first we map the image points from our roughly cylindrical surface onto a plane. For a true cylinder that just means unrolling it, a linear map of the angle around the axis. Our cross-section is a hexagon, so we use an empirical mapping to lay the six image planes out flat.

From there we use affine factorization (Hartley and Zisserman). Subtract each view’s centroid from its image points and stack them into a measurement matrix \(W\) with the views down the rows and the points across the columns. In the affine model

\[ W = M X , \]

where \(M\) stacks the camera matrices and \(X\) holds the 3D points, so \(W\) has rank at most 3. With noise it won’t factor exactly, so we take the rank-3 matrix \(\hat W\) closest to \(W\) in the Frobenius norm. That’s the truncated SVD:

\[ \hat W = U_3 D_3 V_3^{\mathsf T}, \] \[ M = U_3 D_3, \qquad X = V_3^{\mathsf T}. \]

The result is only defined up to an affine transform. For any invertible \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(A\),

\[ \hat W = (M A)(A^{-1} X), \]

so a metric reconstruction needs extra constraints, such as assuming orthographic cameras or using known distances in the scene.

To test the pipeline we start with synthetic data in a flat world. A camera with a circular image surface moves along a straight line between two rows of squares. Its image is reprojected onto two lines, one above the camera and one below, each covering half the circle. With one dimension dropped, the measurement matrix has rank 2 instead of 3.

The reconstruction gets some of the relative structure right but has severe projective distortion. The distortion depends on each point’s depth, so no affine transform can undo it. That calls for projective factorization (Hartley and Zisserman, Algorithm 18.2), which gives each image point an unknown scale \(\lambda_{ij}\),

\[ \lambda_{ij}\,\mathbf{x}_{ij} = P_i\,\mathbf{X}_j , \]

and alternates between factoring and re-estimating the scales. We don’t get it working. We start every \(\lambda_{ij}\) at 1, which isn’t always a good guess, and the factorization reaches rank 3 right away, so the step that updates \(\lambda\) never runs. Lowering the convergence threshold doesn’t help.

Looking back

The project tries to do too much at once. Hardware, calibration, stitching and a new kind of structure from motion could each fill the whole project, and none of them gets as much time as it needs.

On the structure-from-motion side, the camera positions are known in the synthetic test. Given even a rough pose estimate, bundle adjustment could triangulate the points, refine them and recompute the poses. On the real robot, visual SLAM would be the more serious approach, with the motor data helping to localize the camera. The robot also moves very little, and triangulation needs larger motions to be numerically stable. Driving farther brings its own problems: localization has to be better and objects start to occlude each other.
