A 360° camera from six $20 action cameras

April 2023 · Computer vision, Machining, Electronics

For our final project in CMPUT 428, the computer vision course at the University of Alberta, Justin Rozeboom and I set out to build a cheap 360° camera that could drive around on a robot, and to adapt structure from motion to a cylindrical camera in place of the usual pinhole one. I built the camera and the robot setup and did the calibration and stitching; Justin led the reprojection and structure from motion. The code is on GitHub at JustinValentine/panoramic-image-SFM.

The cameras

I already knew action cameras, so I picked cheap ones off AliExpress. The deadline made us choose quickly, and they turned out to be a poor fit. The advertised 110° field of view was about 70° × 40° in practice, and each camera came with a waterproof case, batteries, mounts and a screen on the back, none of which we used. That money could have gone into a better sensor. Small USB cameras would have suited the project better: they advertise wider fields of view, and being smaller they can sit closer together, which helps with parallax.

The bigger job was getting six separate cameras to behave as one. Each had to be switched on and then put into USB camera mode through its menu: 18 button presses for all six. My plan was to wire the on, select and down buttons of all six cameras together so one press would work every camera. Later I found I could have just soldered each on button closed so the camera starts as soon as it gets power, and in the final version the on buttons are shorted by twisting together the wires I’d spent so long attaching. Trying to remove a button from one camera ripped a solder pad off the PCB, so I left the other five alone. Startup went from 18 presses to 7.

Choosing a layout

We tried several layouts first. Each had its own problems, and most needed more cameras to cover the full circle.

We settled on a hexagon with one camera per side, which approximates a cylindrical camera reasonably well and gives neighbouring views enough overlap to stitch. Each camera sits off-centre on its side, placed so that every point where two neighbouring fields of view cross is the same distance from both cameras.

The housing

The housing is laser-cut wood, which is cheap and easy to work with, and the laser cutter places each camera precisely, which matters for stitching. My first cut file had a big mistake: the laser would have cut off every tab. Once that was fixed and the parts were assembled, the result looked like a birdhouse.

Power and driving

The robot is an iRobot Create 2, a low-cost differential-drive robot made for development, which we already had. All six cameras can stream over one USB cable, but together they draw too much power for it, so they run off a powered USB hub fed by one power bank and the cable to the computer carries only data. A Raspberry Pi 3B on the robot, on the second power bank, reads a PS4 controller and sends wheel velocities to the Create 2 over serial, with an SSD alongside for the images and video.

To finish it I painted the wood, sleeved the USB cables, raised the cameras on a cardboard tube and made four mounting points on the robot for the housing.

Calibration

The lenses have strong barrel distortion, so every frame has to be undistorted before stitching. We calibrated with OpenCV from photos of a checkerboard (8 × 6 inner corners) held at different positions and angles.

My first attempt used cv.calibrateCamera(), OpenCV’s standard model, which threw away so much of the image around the edges that stitching would have been impossible. The second used OpenCV’s fisheye module: cv2.fisheye.calibrate() for the intrinsics and cv2.fisheye.estimateNewCameraMatrixForUndistortRectify() to undistort. It took a lot more effort to set up and worked much better.

The fisheye model maps a point \((X, Y, Z)\) in the camera frame to a pixel through the angle \(\theta\) between its ray and the optical axis:

\[ a = \frac{X}{Z}, \qquad b = \frac{Y}{Z}, \] \[ r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \qquad \theta = \arctan r, \] \[ \theta_d = \theta\,\big(1 + k_1\theta^2 + k_2\theta^4 + k_3\theta^6 + k_4\theta^8\big), \] \[ u = f_x\,\frac{\theta_d}{r}\,a + c_x, \qquad v = f_y\,\frac{\theta_d}{r}\,b + c_y . \]

Calibration finds the camera matrix \(K\) (focal lengths and principal point, with skew fixed at zero) and the distortion coefficients \(D = (k_1, k_2, k_3, k_4)\). At 1280 × 720 we got

\[ K = \begin{bmatrix} 841.7 & 0 & 649.7 \\ 0 & 843.3 & 345.2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \] \[ D = (-0.157,\ 0.657,\ -1.178,\ 0.751). \]

Undistorting goes the other way: for each output pixel, OpenCV runs the ray through it back through the model to find where it lands in the raw frame, and samples there. The balance setting, 0.9 in our script, trades how much of the original frame is kept against how much is cropped. The raw frames come out upside down, so the script flips each one first, and it applies the same \(K\) and \(D\) to all six cameras.

To check the result I printed 16 checkerboards, tiled them into one large board and photographed it with one of the cameras. After undistortion the grid lines came out straight.

Stitching

Our first panoramas were lined up by hand in Photoshop and came out poorly. By hand there’s no consistent way to deal with distortion and parallax, or to work out how each frame relates to the next.

OpenCV’s cv2.Stitcher_create() in panorama mode did much better. It detects and matches features between frames, estimates the transformations between them and combines them with multi-band blending, which hides many of the seams that parallax causes.

Parallax is the main limit. The six cameras don’t share an optical centre, so nearby objects shift between neighbouring views and can’t line up exactly, and the Create 2 is built for indoors, where things tend to be close. A simple fix is to turn the robot 30°, half the camera spacing, and capture again. Twelve views overlap far more than six, so the stitcher has more matches to work with, including some that parallax affects less.

Projecting onto a cylinder

The stitcher’s output looked good, but I didn’t know how geometrically accurate it was compared with a true cylindrical camera. The projection types on offer in PhotoStitcher, a photo-stitching program, got me thinking about it, so I implemented the ProjectOntoCylinder function from Kedia Rahul’s “Panorama Stitching” guide and ran every frame through it before stitching.

Each column of the output is an angle around the cylinder, \(\theta = (x' - c_x)/f\), and each row is a height on it. A ray at angle \(\theta\) hits the flat image plane at \(f\tan\theta\) from the centre, and at that angle the plane is \(1/\cos\theta\) times farther away than the cylinder, so heights scale by the same factor. For each output pixel \((x', y')\), the code samples the original image at

\[ x = f\tan\!\left(\frac{x' - c_x}{f}\right) + c_x, \] \[ y = \frac{y' - c_y}{\cos\!\big((x' - c_x)/f\big)} + c_y , \]

with bilinear interpolation, then crops off the empty columns at the sides.

The warp needs the focal length \(f\) in pixels, and converting from the lens focal length in millimetres takes the sensor width. So I took apart a broken camera and measured its sensor with calipers: 3.6 mm wide, covering the 1280-pixel image width, so

\[ f_{\text{px}} = \frac{f_{\text{mm}}}{3.6\ \text{mm}} \times 1280 . \]

Testing the cylindrical model

To measure how well the panoramas match a cylindrical camera, I marked angles on the floor around the robot and heights up a board. The plan was to photograph the board at each angle, stitch, and compare where the marks landed with where the model put them. The walls in that room were too plain, though: the stitcher couldn’t find enough matching features between frames, and I never got a panorama good enough to measure.

Reprojection and structure from motion

Justin Rozeboom led this part. The common structure-from-motion algorithms work with flat image planes, so first we mapped the image points from our roughly cylindrical surface onto a plane. For a true cylinder that just means unrolling it, a linear map of the angle around the axis. Our cross-section is a hexagon, so we used an empirical mapping to lay the six image planes out flat.

From there we used affine factorization (Hartley and Zisserman). Subtract each view’s centroid from its image points and stack them into a measurement matrix \(W\), views down the rows and points across the columns. In the affine model

\[ W = M X , \]

where \(M\) stacks the camera matrices and \(X\) holds the 3D points, so \(W\) has rank at most 3. With noise it won’t factor exactly, so we take the rank-3 matrix \(\hat W\) closest to \(W\) in the Frobenius norm, which is the truncated SVD:

\[ \hat W = U_3 D_3 V_3^{\mathsf T}, \] \[ M = U_3 D_3, \qquad X = V_3^{\mathsf T}. \]

The result is only defined up to an affine transform. For any invertible \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(A\),

\[ \hat W = (M A)(A^{-1} X), \]

so a metric reconstruction needs extra constraints, such as assuming orthographic cameras or using known distances in the scene.

To test the pipeline we started with synthetic data in a flat world. A camera with a circular image surface moves along a straight line between two rows of squares, and its image is reprojected onto two lines, one above the camera and one below, each covering half the circle. With one dimension dropped, the measurement matrix has rank 2 instead of 3.

The reconstruction got some of the relative structure right but had severe projective distortion. That distortion depends on each point’s depth, so no affine transform can undo it. It calls for projective factorization (Hartley and Zisserman, Algorithm 18.2), which gives each image point an unknown scale \(\lambda_{ij}\),

\[ \lambda_{ij}\,\mathbf{x}_{ij} = P_i\,\mathbf{X}_j , \]

and alternates between factoring and re-estimating the scales. We didn’t get it working. We started every \(\lambda_{ij}\) at 1, which isn’t always a good guess, and the factorization reached rank 3 right away, so the step that updates \(\lambda\) never ran. Lowering the convergence threshold didn’t help.

Looking back

The project tried to do too much at once. Hardware, calibration, stitching and a new kind of structure from motion could each have filled it, and none got the time it needed.

In the synthetic test the camera positions were known, and given even a rough pose estimate, bundle adjustment could triangulate the points, refine them and recompute the poses. On the real robot, visual SLAM would be the more serious approach, with the motor data helping to localize the camera. The robot also moved very little, and triangulation needs larger motions to be numerically stable. Driving farther brings its own problems: localization has to be better and objects start to occlude each other.
