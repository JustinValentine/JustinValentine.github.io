Go on a sphere

January 2022 · Games, Software

A flat Go board has edges and corners, and a lot of the game’s strategy comes from them. A sphere has neither. I’ve started on it while taking MATH 348, Differential Geometry of Curves and Surfaces, at the University of Alberta, and so far I’ve drawn a latitude–longitude grid through a stereographic projection in OpenGL.

Drawing a sphere on a screen

This is where the course earns its keep. Gauss’s Theorema Egregium says Gaussian curvature depends only on distances measured within a surface. A sphere of radius \(r\) has curvature \(1/r^2\) everywhere and a plane has zero, so no flat map of a sphere can keep every distance right. Every view of the board has to distort something.

I draw the grid with the stereographic projection. With the sphere at the origin, project from the north pole \((0, 0, 1)\) onto the plane \(z = 0\). A point \((X, Y)\) on the plane comes from the point

\[ (x, y, z) = \frac{\big(2X,\; 2Y,\; X^2 + Y^2 - 1\big)}{X^2 + Y^2 + 1} \]

on the sphere, and in these coordinates the sphere’s metric is

\[ ds^2 = \frac{4\,(dX^2 + dY^2)}{\big(1 + X^2 + Y^2\big)^2}, \]

a single scale factor times the flat metric. So stereographic projection preserves angles and sends circles to circles, which would keep stones round on the map. What it gives up is size: the scale grows without bound towards the north pole, which lands at infinity.

Update, October 2026: a playable game

In October 2026 I came back and finished it as a playable game in C++17 and OpenGL 3.3, with a computer opponent, six ways of viewing the board and tests for the rules.

The board

The board is a latitude–longitude grid: rings evenly spaced between the poles, crossed by meridians. Every crossing is a point, and so are the two poles.

Small6 rings × 14 meridians + 2 poles = 86 points (9×9 has 81)
Medium9 × 20 + 2 = 182 points (13×13 has 169)
Large13 × 28 + 2 = 366 points (19×19 has 361)

Each ring wraps all the way around, so every ring point has exactly four neighbours, two along its ring and two along its meridian. The poles are the odd ones out. A pole touches every point of the nearest ring, so a stone there has as many liberties as there are meridians (20 on the medium board), and capturing it takes a whole ring of stones.

The board still knows it’s a sphere. With \(R\) rings and \(M\) meridians there are \(V = RM + 2\) points, \(E = (2R + 1)M\) edges (along the rings, between neighbouring rings, and out to the poles), and \(F = (R + 1)M\) faces (quadrilaterals between rings, triangles around the poles). Then

\[ V - E + F = (RM + 2) - (2R + 1)M + (R + 1)M = 2, \]

the Euler characteristic of a sphere, for every board size.

Views

The stereographic map is the main view. Because the north pole lands at infinity, the default view puts a globe of the far side next to the map.

There are six views in all: globe plus stereographic map, two globes (front and back), the stereographic map alone, Peirce’s quincuncial projection (which also preserves angles and fits the whole sphere into a square), equirectangular, and Mercator. Dragging rotates the sphere in every view, so you can bring the part of the board you care about into the least distorted region.

Rules

The game uses the Tromp–Taylor rules, which only care about points and adjacency, so they carry over to the sphere unchanged. A group is captured when it can’t reach an empty point, a move can’t repeat an earlier position (positional superko), and the game ends after two passes. The score is stones plus surrounded territory, with 7.5 komi for White.

The computer opponent

The computer runs Monte Carlo tree search with RAVE. Each turn it plays tens of thousands of quick random games from the current position, on every core but one, and steers them towards the moves that have won most often. Each candidate move is scored as

\[ (1 - \beta)\,\frac{w}{n} + \beta\,\tilde q + c\,\sqrt{\frac{\ln N}{n + 1}}, \qquad \beta = \sqrt{\frac{k}{3n + k}}, \]

where \(w/n\) is the move’s own win rate over \(n\) visits, \(\tilde q\) is its RAVE win rate (counted whenever that player played the point at any later time in a random game), \(N\) is the parent’s visit count, \(k = 1000\) and \(c = 0.1\). While \(n\) is small, a move leans on its RAVE estimate; at \(n = 1000\) visits \(\beta = 1/2\), and after that its own results count for more. Like the rules, the search only needs to know which points neighbour which, so the poles get no special treatment.
