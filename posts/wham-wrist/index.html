Building the WHAM wrist

April 7, 2026 · Haptics, Mechanisms, Machining, Electronics

The WHAM wrist is the haptic wrist I built in the first few months of 2026, before I left for Barrett Technology. It mounts on the forearm of a WAM arm that the operator wears as the leader. It came after the coaxial semi-differential wrist and before the final version I designed at Barrett. It was my biggest machining project to date. I’m now writing a paper on it with Martin Jagersand.

The photos run from a printed version on January 23 to the wrist on the WAM on April 7. Each caption has the date the photo or video was taken.

Two haptic joints and a joystick

The coaxial wrist had haptic pronation–supination (PS), flexion–extension (FE) and radial–ulnar deviation (RU), plus a fourth joint that turned the whole wrist. This one has two haptic joints, PS and FE. Their axes cross at the operator’s wrist, so a torque on the handle reaches the hand as a pure moment. They map one to one onto the follower WAM’s J5 and J6.

The WAM’s wrist is roll–pitch–roll, and RU has no matching joint on it. RU bends the hand sideways, while the follower’s J7 spins the tool about its own axis. J7 turns through 344°, and an operator’s roughly 55° of RU would cover about 16% of that. So J7 goes to a thumb joystick on the handle, in rate mode: the stick’s deflection sets J7’s speed, and letting go holds the angle. Dropping RU also takes a motor and a transmission off the far end of the leader.

PS turns ±170° and FE ±90°, both more than a human wrist can manage. The ranges come from the follower, whose J5 travels 344° between its stops and J6 ±90°. The coaxial wrist’s PS range was ±90°.

Attaching it to the arm

The WAM’s upper arm is 550 mm long, against about 330 mm for a 1.75 m adult, so the operator’s elbow and the leader’s can’t line up. In the plane of the arm, the operator and the leader form a closed loop. Kutzbach’s formula gives its mobility from the number of links \(n\) and one-DOF joints \(j\):

\[ M = 3(n-1) - 2j \]

With the cuff bolted straight to the leader’s forearm, the loop is a four-bar: ground, the operator’s upper arm, the joined forearms and the leader’s upper arm. That gives \(M = 3 \cdot 3 - 2 \cdot 4 = 1\). The operator’s shoulder and elbow are then stuck on one path, and moving off it means leaning or hunching against the cuff.

The WHAM wrist has a passive revolute joint between the leader’s forearm and the wrist’s forearm cuff, parallel to the leader’s elbow. That adds a link and a joint:

\[ M = 3(5-1) - 2 \cdot 5 = 2 \]

Two is what the operator’s shoulder and elbow have in that plane, so the operator’s arm sets the leader’s pose. The cost is that the leader’s elbow angle no longer equals the operator’s. The passive joint has an MA600 magnetic encoder, so its angle is known and the wrist’s weight can be compensated in any pose.

There’s a forearm cuff on the wrist and an upper-arm cuff on the biceps. Both are lined with 2 mm of high-density EVA foam and 2 mm of neoprene, with silicone strips that grip the arm, and they slide along carbon-fibre rods to fit different arm lengths.

A printed version first

The first version was printed in black plastic. It already has the layout of the aluminum one: a big drum around the forearm for PS, driven by cable from a pinion on a base plate beside it, with the FE drive mounted on the drum.

Machining the drum

The machined parts are 6061-T6 aluminum. The PS drum was the biggest of them, 185 mm across, driven by an 18 mm pinion in a single cable stage. It started as a round blank on a Tormach PCNC 1100. Four days later one face was pocketed, and by the evening of February 2 the pockets were cut through, leaving the spokes.

The FE drive

A single-stage FE drive with the same ratio as PS would need a drum of about 180 mm beside the wrist. So FE reduces in two stages. An 18 mm motor pinion drives a 36 mm drum, and a 15 mm pinion on the same shaft drives the 77 mm output pulley. There are two of these intermediate shafts, one on each side of the joint.

The motor sits on the FE axis inside the output pulley. The pulley turns on its own bearings around the fixed motor housing, with about 1 mm of radial clearance. The first-stage cables run over the top of the motor pinion and the second-stage cables along the bottom to the output pulley, so the two stages form one closed, preloaded cable loop. Nesting the motor this way shortens the joint, so the wrist can sit lower on the forearm, nearer the operator’s own wrist.

Tensioners inside the pinions

Both joints use 7×19 stainless steel cable (18-8), 0.97 mm across. Each stage is an antagonistic pair wrapped on its pinion. Pretension keeps both sides taut so there’s no backlash, and it has to be high enough that the cable doesn’t slip at peak torque. More than that adds bearing friction and torque ripple, so I wanted a way to set it finely, in place, with the wrist assembled.

The part I’m proudest of is that the tensioners are inside the motor pinions. There’s no turnbuckle or idler anywhere in the cable run. Each motor pinion is split in two, in a scheme similar to the WAM’s own. The fixed half is part of the rotor hub. The tensioning half rides on a one-way needle clutch between two thrust bearings, and a wave spring under the preload screw holds it down. Each half carries eight wraps of one end of the cable.

To tension a drive, I turn the tensioning half against the fixed half. That winds cable onto it and pulls the whole loop tighter. The clutch only lets it turn one way, so it holds whatever tension I reach, and I can add a little at a time until it’s right. Because the clutch sits on the motor shaft, the one adjustment sets the pretension of the whole loop, including both stages of the FE drive.

How much pretension is enough comes from the capstan equation. With a pretension \(T_0\) in both branches, a torque \(\tau\) on a pinion of radius \(r\) raises one branch and lowers the other by \(\tau/2r\). The cable can’t slip as long as the ratio of the two stays under \(e^{\mu\beta}\), where \(\mu\) is the friction coefficient and \(\beta\) the wrap angle:

\[ \frac{T_0 + \tau/2r}{T_0 - \tau/2r} \le e^{\mu\beta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_0 \ge \frac{\tau}{2r}\,\coth\!\left(\frac{\mu\beta}{2}\right). \]

Eight wraps is \(\beta = 16\pi\), so for any friction coefficient of 0.1 or more the \(\coth\) term is within about 1.5% of 1. The wraps make slip a non-issue, and the pretension only has to keep the slack side from going slack. For the PS drive, with the GL60’s 1.75 N·m peak on the 18 mm pinion (9.48 mm to the cable centreline), that’s about 92 N.

The handle

The handle has a two-axis thumb joystick with a click, a bumper button and the adaptive-trigger module from a PlayStation 5 DualSense controller. Inside the module, a small brushed DC motor sets the trigger’s resistance through a worm gear. A Teensy 4 in the handle reads the stick and the button and drives the trigger’s motor. When it isn’t driving J7, the joystick moves the computer’s mouse pointer, so the operator can use the PC without taking the wrist off.

The grip slides on a miniature linear guide along the FE output pulley’s tang, with 25 mm of free travel. That lets the operator centre their wrist on the joint axes, and it keeps the loop through the forearm, wrist and hand from over-constraining.

Reductions and torque

I kept both reductions near 10:1. Backdriven through a reduction \(N\), the motor’s friction feels \(N\) times larger and its rotor inertia \(N^2\) times larger. A low ratio keeps the joints backdrivable enough to estimate torque from motor current.

Each ratio is taken at the cable centreline, so every diameter gets one cable diameter, \(d = 0.965\) mm, added to it. PS has one stage, an 18 mm pinion on the 185 mm drum:

\[ N_{\text{PS}} = \frac{185 + d}{18 + d} = 9.81 \]

FE has two, 18 to 36 mm and then 15 to 77 mm:

\[ N_{\text{FE}} = \frac{36 + d}{18 + d} \cdot \frac{77 + d}{15 + d} = 1.95 \times 4.88 = 9.52 \]

Both joints use a CubeMars GL60 KV25, an outer-rotor gimbal motor rated at 0.6 N·m continuous and 1.75 N·m peak. Through the reductions:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{PS:}\quad & 9.81 \times 0.6 = 5.9\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m}\ \text{rated}, \qquad 9.81 \times 1.75 = 17.2\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m}\ \text{peak} \\ \text{FE:}\quad & 9.52 \times 0.6 = 5.7\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m}\ \text{rated}, \qquad 9.52 \times 1.75 = 16.7\ \mathrm{N{\cdot}m}\ \text{peak} \end{aligned} \]

The rotor’s 355 g·cm² reflects to \(9.81^2 \times 355 \approx 34\) and \(9.52^2 \times 355 \approx 32\) kg·cm² at the joints. The GL60 has 24 slots and 28 poles, so its cogging repeats 168 times per revolution, the least common multiple of the two. At the joint that’s a period of about 0.22°, and the 2.4 cN·m of cogging torque CubeMars gives scales to about 0.23–0.24 N·m.

Testing

In October 2026 I ran my test suite on both joints, and the full results are on that post. PS tracks position to 47 Hz and FE to 31 Hz at −3 dB, and FE has about three times the sliding friction of PS, 0.50 N·m against 0.15 N·m.
