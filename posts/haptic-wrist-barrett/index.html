The Alberta wrist

September 30, 2026 · Haptics, Mechanisms, Machining, Electronics, Control

Finding a haptic wrist design, part 6 of 6. Before I leave for Barrett, I’m trying to settle on a wrist design for our WAM teleoperation setup, so I build and test one idea after another. These are the designs in the order I try them.

2024–2026. MSc thesis, and four months at Barrett Technology.

The Alberta wrist is where my search for a wrist design ended. It mounts on a 4‑DOF WAM arm on the operator’s side, and together they make a 7‑DOF leader for a second WAM, so the operator feels the forces the follower runs into. It came after the WHAM wrist and is the last stage of a research project in our lab that goes back to 2014. In my thesis drafts it’s the P4 wrist, after the Barrett P4-37 Pucks that drive it.

In 2026 I spent four months at Barrett Technology near Boston, the company that makes the WAM, designing this wrist with their engineers. I did the mechanical design and drawings, worked with the manufacturers on tolerances and materials, ran the FEA, and worked through cable stiffness, pulley geometry and reflected inertia.

Across my MSc I designed and built four cable-driven prototypes, one with a precision cable-tensioning unit, machined more than a dozen of the aluminum parts on CNC mills and lathes, and designed a PCB for the handle around a PS5 trigger module. I also wrote the real-time C++ on libbarrett, including a port of Barrett’s gravity calibration tools to the new wrist, and led three undergraduates on it from mechanical engineering, computing science and computer engineering.

Cardboard first

The middle of the wrist is a cable differential. Two motors each turn an input sector, and the sectors drive a pulley that carries the handle. The pulley turns on an axle inside a cage, which pivots on the same axis as the sectors. Turn both sectors the same way and the pulley stays locked while the whole cage tilts: that’s FE. Turn them opposite ways and the cage stays put while the pulley spins on its axle: that’s UR.

Printed prototypes

From June to late July I printed versions in black plastic to work out the ergonomics, and the handle changed the most from one print to the next.

The wrist’s three axes meet at one point, the wrist centre. Two of its three handles are wrist-centred, one wide and one narrow, so the operator’s own wrist sits where the axes cross and a motor torque reaches the hand as a torque, without a sideways push on the forearm. The third is centred on the hand. The handle’s hub sits on the face of the differential pulley, its angle set on a detent plate: four ball-nose spring plungers in the hub sit in the plate’s scallops, with a knurled thumb screw.

The final design

The PS stage is a platform that turns on a large bearing. Inside it is a fixed pulley, and the PS motor, riding on the platform, drives against that pulley with its cable pinion. Four side plates on the platform carry the FE axis, the two input sectors with their motor modules, and the differential cage between them. Each motor module is a TQ ILM50x08 frameless motor in a machined housing, with a hollow cable pinion for a shaft and a Puck P4-37 on the back. The drive cables are 1.12 mm (0.044 in) 7×7×7 tungsten rope, the differential uses 1.37 mm (0.054 in) 7×7×7 stainless, and one-way-clutch tensioners take up the slack.

Machined parts

Barrett had the parts for three wrists made by an outside machine shop, and they were on a bench at Barrett by August 24. The cage, platform, side plates, differential pulley and handle are 6061-T6 aluminum, and the pinion shafts 7075-T6.

The motor modules went together over August 24 and 25.

Putting them together

The wrist sits on a forearm link that attaches at the WAM’s elbow. The link is my own design, printed in PLA, with a passive joint that carries a Puck. In the control software that joint is J5, and the wrist’s PS, FE and UR are J6 to J8.

On the WAM

The arm and the wrist run from an external control PC on the CANopen version of libbarrett, and the Pucks take a torque command on every 500 Hz cycle.

At IROS

IROS 2026 was in Pittsburgh at the end of September, and the wrist went to Barrett’s booth running a lightsaber game I made for it.

Motors and ratios

Three motors drive three joints: PS has its own, and FE and UR share two through the differential. The cable ratios in the commissioned configuration are \(N_2 = 8.7456\) on PS and \(N_1 = 7.5077\) from each differential motor to its sector. With the sign conventions in the control software, the motor angles are

\[ \theta_6 = -N_2\,q_{PS}, \qquad \theta_7 = N_1\,(q_{FE} + q_{UR}), \qquad \theta_8 = N_1\,(q_{UR} - q_{FE}) \]

so FE is the difference of the two differential motors and UR their sum, each divided by \(2N_1\). By virtual work, the joint torques are

\[ \tau_{PS} = -N_2\,\tau_6, \qquad \tau_{FE} = N_1\,(\tau_7 - \tau_8), \qquad \tau_{UR} = N_1\,(\tau_7 + \tau_8) \]

If each motor is limited to \(|\tau_i| \le \tau_m\), FE and UR share a diamond of reachable torques,

\[ |\tau_{FE}| + |\tau_{UR}| \le 2N_1\tau_m \]

so either joint alone gets both motors, and pushing hard on one leaves less for the other. A rotor inertia \(J_r\) shows up at the joints as

\[ J_{PS} = N_2^2 J_r, \qquad J_{FE} = J_{UR} = 2N_1^2 J_r \]

and the cross terms between FE and UR cancel.

The drives are configured with \(K_t = 58\) mN·m/A, a peak current of 16.6 A rms and a continuous 5.17 A rms, about 0.96 and 0.30 N·m per motor. Through the ratios that’s 8.4 N·m peak and 2.6 N·m continuous on PS. FE or UR alone gets 14.5 N·m peak and 4.5 N·m continuous, or 7.2 N·m each when both are at peak together. These numbers come from the drive settings: I haven’t checked them against TQ’s datasheet or measured any torque at the handle yet. On the bench, PS runs out of drive voltage at about 855 °/s, or 1250 rpm at the motor.

The drive configuration lists 56 g·cm² for the rotor, which puts 4.3 kg·cm² on PS and 6.3 on each of FE and UR. Against the measured joint inertias of 138, 31.5 and 22.5 kg·cm², that’s about 3% of PS, a fifth of FE and more than a quarter of UR.

The July study

In early July, before the motors were settled, I wrote a study on choosing the two differential motors and their ratio \(n\), in motor turns per joint turn. Its argument: reflected rotor inertia, \(2n^2J_r\) on each axis, is the one parasitic a controller can’t remove, while cogging and friction grow only as \(n\) and can be partly compensated, so \(n\) should be as small as the torque requirements allow. Peak and continuous torque set a floor, and the ceiling is how much inertia I can add to the operator’s hand without them noticing:

\[ n_{\min} = \max\left\{\frac{\hat\tau}{2\tau_{pk}},\ \frac{\bar\tau_{FE} + \bar\tau_{UR}}{2\tau_{rated}}\right\}, \qquad n_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{\beta\, I_{hand}}{2J_r}} \]

The requirements were a peak \(\hat\tau\) of 7 N·m per axis and 2.5 N·m continuous on both axes at once. The peak is the mean maximum wrist moment in extension, the weakest direction, from Delp et al. (1996). The study takes the hand’s inertia about the wrist as \(I_{hand} = 2.5\times10^{-3}\) kg·m² and caps the added inertia at \(\beta = 0.10\) of it, under the Weber fraction of about 0.11 for moment of inertia reported by Kreifeldt and Chuang (1979).

Of the four CubeMars motors it compared, only the frameless RI50 and RI60 had a window. For the RI50 (1.67 N·m peak, 0.58 N·m rated, 22.8 g·cm²) the continuous-torque floor is 4.31 and the ceiling 7.40, and the study picked \(n = 5\), where the two rotors add \(1.14\times10^{-4}\) kg·m², 4.6% of the hand. The GL60 gimbal motor’s 355 g·cm² rotor puts its ceiling at 1.88, under its own peak-torque floor of 2.00, and the smaller GL40 needs \(n = 10\) for the continuous torque. Two checks were left for hardware: cogging, which CubeMars doesn’t publish for the RI motors, and whether their currents suit the Puck.

The RI50 never made it into the wrist. Its winding topology means the Puck can’t control it, so the final wrist uses TQ ILM50x08 frameless motors, which the P4-37 drives, at the ratios above.

FEA and a simulated wrist

I checked the structure with linear FEA, first in a July design study and again on the final parts for my thesis. The thesis runs put five loads on the wrist centre: 100 N down, 100 N along the FE axis, 100 N along the UR axis, and 9.1 N·m about the FE axis and about the PS axis. The 6061-T6 in the cage, platform and side plates yields at 276 MPa.

The PS platform and its four side plates peak at 39 MPa, a safety factor of 7, under the push along the FE axis, which bends the plates across their 5.5 mm thickness and moves the wrist centre 355 µm. In the other cases it moves 15 µm or less. The 7075-T6 pinion shafts sit some 40 times under their yield strength at a 116 N cable load. Steel would cut the pinion-tip deflection from 3.8 to 1.4 µm, but at 98 g against aluminum’s 35.

The handle is the weak part. Its two side arms are 10 mm deep but only 1.8 mm thick where they run diagonally to the hub, so they twist as well as bend. Under the same five loads the arms peak at 199, 381, 317, 348 and 408 MPa, and only the 100 N push down stays under yield, with a safety factor of 1.4. The analysis is linear, so past yield it shows a trend and nothing more.

There’s a simulated wrist too: each experiment in my test suite can run against emulated Pucks on a virtual CAN bus, with the real control library, before it touches the hardware.

Other work at Barrett

At Barrett I also worked on the software and tools around the wrist:

  • Python interfaces for Barrett’s motor controllers, and tools for gravity calibration, dynamics identification and friction compensation.
  • Automated motor tests on a dynamometer: load sweeps, efficiency curves, and thermal and peak-torque tests.
  • Work on porting the WAM from Barrett’s own CAN protocol to standard CANopen, from the control library down to the safety-board firmware.
  • The lightsaber game for Barrett’s booth at IROS 2026.

For the wrist itself I also wrote a real-time C++ CANopen driver, plus the test suite to measure it.

My work on wrists started in my undergrad, when I designed and tested spherical-parallel and serial-gimbal wrist mechanisms with researchers at Johns Hopkins University, under a $500K CFI JELF grant.

More: four months at Barrett on LinkedIn, and the sensorless four-channel teleoperation paper (submitted to IROS 2026).

Update, October 2026: measured results

In October I measured one of the wrists with my test suite, which has the details. The position loop’s −3 dB bandwidth was 33.2, 37.7 and 40.4 Hz on PS, FE and UR. PS had the most friction, 361 mN·m once it was sliding, against 65 on FE and 143 on UR, and its cable showed up as an anti-resonance at 98 Hz, from a cable stiffness of 5327 N·m/rad.
