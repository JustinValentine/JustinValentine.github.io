2024–2026. MSc thesis, and four months at Barrett Technology.

The Alberta wrist is the last haptic wrist in this series. It mounts on a 4‑DOF WAM arm on the operator’s side. Together they make a 7‑DOF leader for a second WAM, and the operator feels the forces the follower runs into. It’s the last stage of a research project in our lab that started in 2014. In my thesis drafts it’s the P4 wrist, after the Barrett P4-37 Pucks that drive it.

During my MSc I designed and built four cable-driven prototypes, including one with a precision cable-tensioning unit. I machined more than a dozen of the aluminum parts on CNC mills and lathes, designed a PCB for the handle around a PS5 trigger module, and wrote the real-time C++ on libbarrett, including a port of Barrett’s gravity calibration tools to the new wrist. I also led three undergraduates who worked on it, from mechanical engineering, computing science and computer engineering. The prototype just before this one was the WHAM wrist.

In 2026 I spent four months at Barrett Technology near Boston, the company that makes the WAM, designing this wrist with their engineers. I did the mechanical design and drawings, worked with the manufacturers on tolerances and materials, ran FEA under several load cases, and worked through cable stiffness, pulley geometry and reflected inertia. Barrett had the parts machined for three wrists.

The photos run from a cardboard model on May 26 to Barrett’s booth at IROS on September 30. Captions give the date, and the local time for phone photos.

Cardboard first

The middle of the wrist is a cable differential. Two motors each turn an input sector, and the two sectors drive a pulley that carries the handle. The pulley turns on an axle inside a cage, and the cage pivots on the same axis as the sectors. When both sectors turn the same way, the pulley stays locked in the cage and the whole cage tilts. That’s FE. When they turn opposite ways, the cage stays where it is and the pulley turns on its axle. That’s UR. The cardboard model is the earliest one I have a photo of.

Printed prototypes

From June to late July I printed versions in black plastic to work on the ergonomics. The handle changed the most from one to the next.

The three axes of the wrist meet at one point, the wrist centre. The wrist comes with three handles. Two are wrist-centred, one wide and one narrow, so the operator’s own wrist sits where the axes cross, and the third is centred on the hand. With the axes through the operator’s wrist, a torque from the motors reaches the hand as a torque, without a sideways push on the forearm. The handle’s hub sits on the face of the differential pulley, and its angle is set on a detent plate there: four ball-nose spring plungers in the hub sit in the plate’s scallops, with a knurled thumb screw.

The final design

The PS stage is a platform that turns on a large bearing. Inside it is a fixed pulley, and the PS motor, which rides on the platform, drives against that pulley with its cable pinion. Four side plates on the platform carry the FE axis, the two input sectors with their motor modules, and the differential cage between them. Each motor module is a TQ ILM50x08 frameless motor in a machined housing, with a hollow cable pinion for a shaft and a Puck P4-37 on the back. The drive cables are 1.12 mm (0.044 in) 7×7×7 tungsten rope, and the differential uses 1.37 mm (0.054 in) 7×7×7 stainless. One-way-clutch tensioners take up the slack.

Machined parts

The parts came from an outside machine shop and were on a bench at Barrett by August 24. The cage, platform, side plates, differential pulley and handle are 6061-T6 aluminum, and the pinion shafts are 7075-T6.

The motor modules went together over August 24 and 25.

Putting them together

The wrist sits on a forearm link that attaches at the WAM’s elbow. I designed the link and printed it in PLA. The link has a passive joint with a Puck on it, which is J5 in the control software, and the wrist’s PS, FE and UR are J6 to J8.

On the WAM

The arm and the wrist run from an external control PC on the CANopen version of libbarrett. The Pucks take a torque command on every 500 Hz cycle.

At IROS

IROS 2026 was in Pittsburgh at the end of September, and the wrist went to Barrett’s booth running a lightsaber game I made for it.

Motors and ratios

Three motors drive three joints. PS has its own, and FE and UR share two through the differential. The cable ratios in the commissioned configuration are \(N_2 = 8.7456\) on PS and \(N_1 = 7.5077\) from each differential motor to its sector. With the sign conventions in the control software, the motor angles are

\[ \theta_6 = -N_2\,q_{PS}, \qquad \theta_7 = N_1\,(q_{FE} + q_{UR}), \qquad \theta_8 = N_1\,(q_{UR} - q_{FE}) \]

so FE is the difference of the two differential motors and UR their sum, each divided by \(2N_1\). By virtual work, the joint torques are

\[ \tau_{PS} = -N_2\,\tau_6, \qquad \tau_{FE} = N_1\,(\tau_7 - \tau_8), \qquad \tau_{UR} = N_1\,(\tau_7 + \tau_8) \]

If each motor is limited to \(|\tau_i| \le \tau_m\), FE and UR share a diamond of reachable torques,

\[ |\tau_{FE}| + |\tau_{UR}| \le 2N_1\tau_m \]

so either joint alone gets both motors, and pushing hard on one leaves less for the other. A rotor inertia \(J_r\) shows up at the joints as

\[ J_{PS} = N_2^2 J_r, \qquad J_{FE} = J_{UR} = 2N_1^2 J_r \]

and the cross terms between FE and UR cancel.

The drives are configured with \(K_t = 58\) mN·m/A, a peak current of 16.6 A rms and a continuous 5.17 A rms, about 0.96 and 0.30 N·m per motor. Through the ratios that’s 8.4 N·m peak and 2.6 N·m continuous on PS. FE or UR alone gets 14.5 N·m peak and 4.5 N·m continuous, or 7.2 N·m each when both are at peak together. These numbers come from the drive settings, which haven’t been checked against TQ’s datasheet, and no torque has been measured at the handle yet. On the bench, PS ran out of drive voltage at about 855 °/s, or 1250 rpm at the motor.

The drive configuration lists 56 g·cm² for the rotor. That puts 4.3 kg·cm² on PS, against the 138 kg·cm² measured for the whole joint, and 6.3 kg·cm² on each of FE and UR, against 31.5 and 22.5 measured. The rotors are about 3% of PS’s inertia, a fifth of FE’s and more than a quarter of UR’s.

The July study

In early July, before the motors were settled, I wrote a study on choosing the two differential motors and their ratio \(n\), in motor turns per joint turn. Its argument is that reflected rotor inertia, \(2n^2J_r\) on each axis, is the one parasitic a controller can’t remove, while cogging and friction grow only as \(n\) and can be partly compensated. So \(n\) should be as small as the torque requirements allow. Peak and continuous torque set a floor, and a limit on how much inertia can be added to the operator’s hand without them noticing sets a ceiling:

\[ n_{\min} = \max\left\{\frac{\hat\tau}{2\tau_{pk}},\ \frac{\bar\tau_{FE} + \bar\tau_{UR}}{2\tau_{rated}}\right\}, \qquad n_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{\beta\, I_{hand}}{2J_r}} \]

The requirements were a peak \(\hat\tau\) of 7 N·m per axis, which the study took from Delp et al. (1996) as the mean maximum wrist moment in extension, the weakest direction, and 2.5 N·m continuous on both axes at once. It took the hand’s inertia about the wrist as \(I_{hand} = 2.5\times10^{-3}\) kg·m² and kept the added inertia to \(\beta = 0.10\) of it, under the Weber fraction of about 0.11 for moment of inertia reported by Kreifeldt and Chuang (1979).

Of the four CubeMars motors it compared, only the frameless RI50 and RI60 had a window. For the RI50 (1.67 N·m peak, 0.58 N·m rated, 22.8 g·cm²) the continuous-torque floor is 4.31 and the ceiling 7.40, and the study picked \(n = 5\), where the two rotors add \(1.14\times10^{-4}\) kg·m², 4.6% of the hand. The GL60 gimbal motor’s 355 g·cm² rotor puts its ceiling at 1.88, under its own peak-torque floor of 2.00, and the smaller GL40 needed \(n = 10\) for the continuous torque. The study left two checks for hardware: cogging, which CubeMars didn’t publish for the RI motors, and whether their currents suit the Puck. The wrist that was built uses TQ ILM50x08 frameless motors at the ratios above.

The final wrist doesn’t use the RI50, though. The RI50 can’t be controlled by the Puck because of its winding topology, so the wrist uses TQ ILM50x08 frameless motors, which the P4-37 Puck drives.

FEA and a simulated wrist

I checked the structure with linear FEA, first in a July design study and again on the final parts for my thesis. The thesis runs put five loads on the wrist centre: 100 N down, 100 N along the FE axis, 100 N along the UR axis, and 9.1 N·m about the FE axis and about the PS axis. The cage, platform and side plates are 6061-T6, with a yield strength of 276 MPa.

The PS platform and its four side plates peak at 39 MPa, a safety factor of 7, under the push along the FE axis, which bends the plates across their 5.5 mm thickness and moves the wrist centre 355 µm. In the other cases it moves 15 µm or less. The pinion shafts are 7075-T6. Under a 116 N cable load, a shaft’s peak stress is about 12 MPa, some 40 times under its yield strength, and the tip of the pinion deflects 3.8 µm. A steel shaft would deflect 1.4 µm and weigh 98 g, against 35 g for the aluminum one.

The handle is the weak part. Its two side arms are 10 mm deep but only 1.8 mm thick where they run diagonally to the hub, so they twist as well as bend. Under the same five loads the peaks in the arms are 199, 381, 317, 348 and 408 MPa. Only the 100 N push down stays under the 276 MPa yield strength of 6061-T6, with a safety factor of 1.4. The analysis is linear, so where it passes yield it shows the trend and not what the part would actually do.

The test code has a simulated wrist too. Each experiment in the test suite can run first against emulated Pucks on a virtual CAN bus, with the real control library, before it touches the hardware.

Measured results

In October I measured one of the wrists on a bench with my test suite, which has the details. Moved by hand into its stops, PS turns 171.8°, FE 131.4° and UR 69.2°. The inertia at the joints is 138.0, 31.5 and 22.5 kg·cm², and the position loop’s −3 dB bandwidth is 33.2, 37.7 and 40.4 Hz. PS has the most friction, 361 mN·m once it’s sliding, against 65 on FE and 143 on UR. On PS the cable shows up as an anti-resonance at 98 Hz, from a cable stiffness of 5327 N·m/rad.

Other work at Barrett

At Barrett I also worked on the software and tools around the wrist:

  • Python interfaces for Barrett’s motor controllers, and tools for gravity calibration, dynamics identification and friction compensation.
  • Automated motor tests on a dynamometer: load sweeps, efficiency curves, and thermal and peak-torque tests.
  • Work on porting the WAM from Barrett’s own CAN protocol to standard CANopen, from the control library down to the safety-board firmware.
  • The lightsaber game for Barrett’s booth at IROS 2026.

For the wrist I also wrote a real-time C++ CANopen driver, and the test suite to measure it.

Before grad school, as an undergraduate, I designed and tested spherical-parallel and serial-gimbal wrist mechanisms with researchers at Johns Hopkins University, under a $500K CFI JELF grant.
