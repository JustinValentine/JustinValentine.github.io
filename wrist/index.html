A haptic wrist for the WAM

The Alberta wrist. Built with Barrett Technology and demonstrated at IROS 2026 in Pittsburgh.

The Alberta wrist is a force-feedback wrist for the Barrett WAM robot arm. On the end of a four-joint WAM it completes a seven-joint haptic leader: an operator holds the handle and moves their own arm, a second robot arm copies the motion, and the leader pushes back with the forces that robot meets. The wrist’s three axes cross at the operator’s own wrist, so a contact at the far robot’s tool arrives as a torque about the wrist.

In action

At IROS 2026 in Pittsburgh, hundreds of visitors tried the wrist at Barrett Technology’s booth, in a lightsaber game I wrote for it. The wrist’s handle is the hilt. The arm sets where the hilt is and the wrist sets where the blade points. When the blades meet, the arm and the wrist push back, harder for a hit near the hilt than for one near the tip.

The game runs inside the arm’s 500 Hz control loop, and its forces come from a model of two blades that can’t pass through each other. I go through how it works in A haptic lightsaber game.

What it’s for

Teleoperation means a person drives a robot from somewhere else. With force feedback the link runs both ways: the operator’s arm moves the robot, and what the robot touches pushes back on the operator. That helps wherever a job needs a person’s judgement and hands but the person can’t or shouldn’t be there, and it’s a way to show a robot how to do a task by doing the task through it.

Our setup uses two WAMs. The WAM is a cable-driven robot arm made by Barrett Technology, about the size of a human arm and light enough on its joints to be pushed around by hand. The operator’s arm rides along one of them, the leader, and the other, the follower, does the work.

The wrist is the hard part of a leader arm, for three reasons. It’s where the hand meets the machine, so it’s where a contact finally has to be felt. It hangs at the far end of the arm, so every gram of motor there is weight the operator swings around with their whole arm. And a human wrist doesn’t bend the way a robot’s does: ours rolls, pitches and then yaws, while the WAM’s rolls, pitches and rolls again.

A wrist for this job also has three things to do that pull against each other. It should stay out of the way while the follower moves freely, keep up with the operator’s own wrist, and feel solid when the follower touches something.

How it works

Three axes through your wrist

The wrist moves the way a human wrist does. Pronation–supination (PS) is the forearm’s roll, as in turning a doorknob. Flexion–extension (FE) bends the hand toward the palm or the back of the hand. Ulnar–radial deviation (UR) tilts it sideways, toward the little finger or the thumb.

The operator’s forearm passes through the large ring bearing at the base, and the hand closes on the handle above it. All three axes cross at one point, and the handle puts the operator’s own wrist joint on that point. This decides what the force feedback feels like. If a device pivots about the handle instead, the wrist joint has to swing along an arc whenever the hand bends, the forearm gets dragged along with it, and a torque from the motors turns partly into a shove along the forearm. With the axes through the wrist, a torque from the motors arrives as a torque.

The wrist comes with three handles. Two are wrist-centred, one wide and one narrow, and the third is centred on the hand. The handle’s angle clicks into place on a detent plate and locks with a thumb screw.

Cables in place of gears

Each joint is driven by a cable wound on a small pinion on its motor’s shaft, the same kind of transmission the WAM itself uses. A cable drive has no backlash, and the reductions here are low: 8.75:1 on PS and 7.51:1 on the other two joints. A low ratio keeps the motors easy to turn from the handle side, which is what lets the operator feel the follower and not the wrist’s own drive.

A differential for two of the joints

PS has a motor to itself. FE and UR share two motors through a cable differential. Each motor turns an input sector, and the two sectors drive a pulley that carries the handle. When both sectors turn the same way, the pulley stays locked in its cage and the whole cage tilts, which is FE. When they turn opposite ways, the cage stays put and the pulley turns on its axle, which is UR. Either motion gets the torque of both motors, and neither motor has to ride on the other’s joint.

Three more joints on the WAM’s own bus

Each motor is a TQ ILM50x08 frameless motor in a machined housing, with a hollow cable pinion for a shaft and a Barrett Puck P4-37 drive on its back. That’s the WAM’s own drive platform, so the wrist joins the arm’s CAN bus as three more joints. It runs under the same control library, libbarrett, with the WAM’s safety board watching it, and it takes a new torque command every 2 ms.

Between the WAM’s elbow and the wrist is a forearm link with one free joint that has a sensor but no motor. A WAM’s upper arm is 550 mm long, against about 330 mm for an adult’s, and the free joint takes up the difference, so the operator’s shoulder and elbow aren’t forced onto the robot’s path.

The cage, platform, side plates, pulley and handle are 6061-T6 aluminum, and the pinion shafts are 7075-T6. The design and build post has the sections, the FEA and the motor and ratio choices.

What I’ve measured

In October 2026 I put the wrist on a bench and ran every joint through a set of experiments for friction, inertia, bandwidth, large motions and rendered contact. Each joint was tested with its axis vertical, so gravity put no torque on it.

  • It’s light to move. The inertia at the joints is 138 kg·cm² on PS, 31.5 on FE and 22.5 on UR. On FE and UR that’s comparable to the hand’s own inertia about the wrist, and on every axis it’s at or below OpenWrist, a comparable research wrist exoskeleton.
  • It keeps up. The position loop follows commands up to 33 to 40 Hz. PS follows a ±10° sinusoid up to 8.3 Hz, and FE and UR up to at least 14.9 and 13.8 Hz, where the test reached its own limits before the wrist did.
  • It’s strong enough to matter. The design target was 7 N·m of peak torque on each axis, the average maximum wrist strength of young men in extension, their weakest direction. The drives allow 8.4 N·m on PS and 14.5 N·m on FE or UR alone.
  • PS has the most friction. It slides at 361 mN·m, against 65 on FE and 143 on UR.
  • Delay sets how stiff a contact can feel. Through the 500 Hz loop on the control PC, where 3 to 4 ms pass between a measurement and the torque it produces, PS holds a virtual spring of 327 N·m/rad, and FE and UR hold 33 and 38. In the Puck’s own position loop, with light damping, PS stays stable past 2000.

That last result is the main finding of my thesis, which compares this wrist with the WHAM wrist that came before it. The stiffness of the contact a leader wrist can render is set by the delay of the loop that renders it, working against the joint’s friction, and not by the wrist’s inertia or its torque.

The torques above come from the drives’ own current measurement. I haven’t checked them against an outside sensor yet, and the tests that need one (torque calibration, cable stiffness, continuous torque and transparency with a hand on the handle) are still to run. Every number, plot and method is in A test suite for the haptic wrist, and the code is on GitHub.

Fourteen years in the making

Our lab has worked on force-feedback teleoperation for fourteen years, and on wrists for the leader arm since 2014. The Alberta wrist comes after about a dozen prototypes.

  • 2003–2005Before our lab’s work, Barrett Technology builds a three-axis gimbal for the end of its WAM, as a teleoperation master for demolition work in nuclear settings (their report to the US Department of Energy). Later versions appear in rehabilitation and DARPA demos. It’s the starting point for our wrists.
  • 2014A new way of mounting the WAM lets it work as an approximate exoskeleton, with the operator’s arm riding along it. One- and two-joint wrists follow, prototyped in wood, with a wooden capstan.
  • 2016A three-joint wooden wrist, with sensors on its joints.
  • 2018–2019A group of mechanical engineering students builds the first cable differential and actuates it.
  • 2021–2024A second cable differential, later moved to moteus motor controllers. In 2024 it’s the wrist on the leader for our haptic mobile manipulator, which opens a door and turns the valve on a venting pipe by teleoperation for DND’s IDEaS challenge.
  • 2023I try a spherical parallel wrist after Laval’s Agile Eye.
  • 2024A direct-drive gimbal, to test strapping the WAM to the operator’s forearm with and without a passive joint.
  • 2025Three more: a direct-drive wrist held like an end effector, a fully actuated wooden wrist with single-stage cable drives, and the compact coaxial semi-differential wrist.
  • 2026The WHAM wrist, with two haptic joints and a thumb joystick, machined in aluminum. Then four months at Barrett Technology and the Alberta wrist.

Getting one

The Alberta wrist is available for purchase. If you’d like one for your WAM, email me at justin@justinvalentine.ca.

The wrist runs on a WAM whose Pucks speak CANopen. Three pieces of software have to match: CANopen firmware on the P4-37 Pucks, safety board firmware rev 202 or later, and the CANopen branch of libbarrett on an external control PC. So far it’s been run from a PC with Ubuntu 20.04 and a PEAK PCAN-USB adapter, and it hasn’t been tested with the WAM’s embedded PC/104. The control loop is fixed at 500 Hz. A user manual is in draft.
