A haptic wrist for the WAM

The Alberta wrist. Built with Barrett Technology and demonstrated at IROS 2026 in Pittsburgh.

The Alberta wrist is a force-feedback wrist for the Barrett WAM robot arm. It mounts on a four-joint WAM on the operator’s side, and together they make a seven-joint leader for a second WAM. The operator holds the handle and moves their arm, the second arm follows, and the operator feels the forces it runs into, at the wrist as well as at the shoulder and elbow.

At IROS 2026

At IROS 2026 in Pittsburgh the wrist was at Barrett Technology’s booth, running a lightsaber game I wrote for it. Hundreds of visitors tried it. The wrist’s handle is the hilt. The arm sets where the hilt is and the wrist sets where the blade points. When your blade meets the droid’s, the arm and the wrist push back, and how hard depends on where along the blade the hit lands.

The game and its haptics run inside the arm’s 500 Hz control loop. A haptic lightsaber game goes through how the forces are computed.

Teleoperation with force feedback

Our lab works on teleoperation with force feedback. A person moves one robot arm, a second arm copies it, and whatever the second arm touches pushes back on the person. It’s meant for work in places too dangerous to send someone. In 2024 a robot of ours, driven this way with an earlier wrist, opened a door and turned the valve on a venting pipe for DND’s IDEaS challenge.

Both arms are WAMs. The WAM is a cable-driven arm made by Barrett Technology, about the size of a human arm, and its joints turn easily enough that you can push it around by hand. The operator’s arm rides along one WAM, the leader, and the other WAM, the follower, does the work.

The wrist is the hardest part of the leader to get right. Its motors hang at the far end of the arm, where the operator carries every gram of them. A human wrist rolls, pitches and yaws, while the WAM’s wrist rolls, pitches and rolls again, so one doesn’t map cleanly onto the other. And the wrist has to stay out of the operator’s way while the follower moves freely and still feel solid when it touches something.

The mechanism

The axes

The wrist has the three motions of a human wrist. Pronation–supination (PS) is the roll of the forearm, as in turning a doorknob. Flexion–extension (FE) bends the hand forward and back, and ulnar–radial deviation (UR) tilts it from side to side.

The operator’s forearm goes through the ring bearing at the base, and the hand holds the handle above it. The three axes meet at one point, and the handle is placed so that the operator’s wrist joint sits there. If the axes passed through the handle instead, the wrist joint would have to travel on an arc every time the hand bent. That drags the forearm along with it, and part of every motor torque is felt as a push on the forearm. With the axes through the wrist, a torque from the motors reaches the hand as a torque.

There are three handles. Two are wrist-centred, one wide and one narrow, and the third is centred on the hand. The handle’s angle clicks into place on a detent plate and locks with a thumb screw.

The cable drives

The joints are driven by cable, like the WAM’s own. Each motor’s shaft is a small grooved pinion, and a wire rope runs from it to a larger pulley or sector at the joint. There are no gears, so there’s no backlash. The ratios are low, 8.75:1 on PS and 7.51:1 on FE and UR, because the operator feels the motor’s friction multiplied by the ratio and its rotor inertia multiplied by the ratio squared.

The differential

PS has its own motor. FE and UR share two through a cable differential. Each of the two motors turns an input sector, and the two sectors drive a pulley that carries the handle. The pulley turns on an axle inside a cage, and the cage pivots on the same axis as the sectors. When both sectors turn the same way, the pulley stays locked in the cage and the whole cage tilts. That’s FE. When they turn opposite ways, the cage stays where it is and the pulley turns on its axle. That’s UR. So either joint alone gets both motors, and pushing hard on one leaves less for the other.

Motors and drives

Each motor is a TQ ILM50x08 frameless motor in a machined housing, with the cable pinion for a shaft and a Barrett Puck P4-37 on the back. Pucks are the drives the WAM itself uses, so the wrist joins the arm’s CAN bus as three more joints and runs under the same control library, libbarrett, and the same safety board. The Pucks take a torque command on every 500 Hz cycle.

The forearm link

The wrist sits on a forearm link that attaches at the WAM’s elbow. The WAM’s upper arm is 550 mm long, against about 330 mm for a 1.75 m adult, so the operator’s elbow and the WAM’s can’t line up. The link has a passive joint to take up the difference, with a Puck on it to read its angle. Without it the operator would have to lean or hunch to follow the arm.

The cage, platform, side plates, differential pulley and handle are 6061-T6 aluminum, and the pinion shafts are 7075-T6. The sections, the FEA and the motor and ratio choices are in the design and build post.

Bench tests

In October 2026 I measured the wrist on a bench, one joint at a time, with the joint’s axis vertical so that gravity put no torque on it.

Moved by hand into its stops, PS turns 172°, FE 131° and UR 69°. The inertia at the joints is 138, 31.5 and 22.5 kg·cm². For FE and UR that’s close to the inertia of the hand itself, and all three are at or below the figures for OpenWrist, a research wrist exoskeleton. PS has the most friction, 361 mN·m once it’s sliding, against 65 on FE and 143 on UR.

The position loop’s −3 dB bandwidth is 33, 38 and 40 Hz. Bandwidth only uses small motions, so I also measured how large a motion each joint can follow. PS tracks a ±10° sinusoid up to 8.3 Hz. FE and UR got to 14.9 and 13.8 Hz before the test hit its own speed and range caps, so their real limits are higher.

The torque target was 7 N·m of peak torque per axis, the average maximum strength of a young man’s wrist in extension, its weakest direction. The drives’ current limit allows 8.4 N·m on PS and 14.5 N·m on FE or UR alone. I haven’t measured torque at the handle yet, so every torque here comes from the drives’ own current measurement.

The main finding of my thesis is about how stiff a contact the wrist can render. Through the 500 Hz loop on the control PC, PS holds a virtual spring of 327 N·m/rad with no damping, and FE and UR hold 33 and 38. Inside the Puck’s own position loop, PS is still stable past 2000. The difference is delay. In the PC loop, 3 to 4 ms pass between a measurement and the torque it produces. The thesis compares this wrist with the WHAM wrist before it and finds that this delay, set against the joint’s friction, is what limits the stiffness, and that the wrist’s inertia and torque are not.

Four tests need a torque sensor at the handle or the output clamped, and I haven’t run them yet: torque calibration, cable stiffness, continuous torque, and transparency with a person moving the handle. The numbers, plots and methods are all in A test suite for the haptic wrist, and the code is on GitHub.

Fourteen years in the making

Our lab has worked on force-feedback teleoperation for fourteen years, and on wrists for the leader arm since 2014. The Alberta wrist comes after about a dozen prototypes.

  • 2003–2005Barrett Technology built a three-axis gimbal for the end of the WAM, as the master of a teleoperation system for demolition work in nuclear settings (their report to the US Department of Energy). Later versions were shown in rehabilitation and DARPA demos. Our wrists start from it.
  • 2014A new way of mounting the WAM let it work as an approximate exoskeleton, with the operator’s arm riding along it. The first wrists were one- and two-joint prototypes in wood, with a wooden capstan.
  • 2016A three-joint wooden wrist with sensors on its joints.
  • 2018–2019A group of mechanical engineering students built and actuated the first cable differential.
  • 2021–2024A second cable differential, later moved to moteus motor controllers. In 2024 it was the wrist on the leader for our haptic mobile manipulator in DND’s IDEaS challenge.
  • 2023A spherical parallel wrist after Laval’s Agile Eye.
  • 2024A direct-drive gimbal, for testing how to strap the WAM to the operator’s forearm, with and without a passive joint.
  • 2025A direct-drive wrist held like an end effector, a fully actuated wooden wrist with single-stage cable drives, and the coaxial semi-differential wrist.
  • 2026The WHAM wrist, with two haptic joints and a thumb joystick, and then the Alberta wrist, which I designed over four months at Barrett Technology.

Buying one

The Alberta wrist is available for purchase. If you’d like one for your WAM, email me at justin@justinvalentine.ca.

It needs a WAM whose Pucks run CANopen firmware. The safety board needs firmware rev 202 or later, and the control PC needs the CANopen branch of libbarrett. So far it has only been run from an external PC, with Ubuntu 20.04 and a PEAK PCAN-USB adapter, and not from the WAM’s embedded PC/104. The control loop is fixed at 500 Hz. A user manual is in draft.
